# Techniques

Pick from the goal for a batch. When the user is stuck, pick by symptom instead:

| Symptom | Technique |
|---|---|
| Ideas all feel the same | Random stimulus |
| A constraint feels unbreakable | Provocation |
| Requirements assume things nobody questioned | Inversion |
| We might be solving the wrong problem | Concept fan |
| It works but feels derivative | Cross-domain analogy |
| One idea, need variants | SCAMPER |
| Everyone agrees too fast | Six hats |
| Everything feels timid or safe | Worst idea |
| Too many moving parts to see options | Morphological analysis |
| "That's just how it's done" | First principles |

## Inversion
List 5–8 assumptions behind the obvious approach. Flip each to its strongest plausible opposite.
Ask where each flip is already true and who benefits from it today. Develop survivors; mark dead
flips dead.
Good when: requirements rest on assumptions nobody has questioned.

## Constraint removal
"What if budget, time, tech or politics weren't factors?" Generate freely, then for each idea ask
what the smallest real-world version is.
Good when: ideas are being killed by feasibility before they're formed.

## Constraint ladder
Three rounds: current real constraints; one negotiable constraint removed; one stricter constraint
added (half the budget, a day not a month, must work offline). Compare which ideas survive each.
Good when: you need to know which constraints actually drive the answer.

## Cross-domain analogy
State the problem's structure in one sentence (actors, flows, bottleneck). Pick 3–5 structurally
similar but distant domains. Map the roles and transfer what each domain *does* about the
bottleneck: mechanisms, not looks.
Good when: the solution works but feels derivative.

## SCAMPER
Run the existing idea through Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify (10x bigger, smaller, faster),
Put to other use, Eliminate, Reverse. Answer only the operations that bite; show empty ones empty.
Good when: there's one idea and you need variants.

## Random stimulus
Pick 8–12 unrelated things across at least five categories (an object, a profession, a place, an
animal, a historical event). List each one's properties and force a connection to the topic. Keep
an idea only if you couldn't have reached it without the stimulus.
Good when: output is predictable and every idea feels the same.

## Provocation
State 4–6 deliberately wrong assertions: escape a rule, reverse a relationship, exaggerate a
quantity, distort the sequence, assert a wish as fact ("customers pay us to wait"). From each,
extract movement: the principle inside it and what would differ from today.
Good when: a constraint or rule feels unbreakable.

## Concept fan
Ask "what is this a way of doing?" twice to climb to broader concepts. At each level, fan out 3–4
alternative concepts that serve the level above, then drop back to concrete ideas. Prune branches
that are the original idea repainted.
Good when: you might be solving the wrong problem.

## Worst idea
Design 5–8 genuinely terrible solutions, each plausible enough that someone has shipped it. Name
the mechanism that makes each one bad, then invert the *mechanism*, not the idea, into a feature.
Good when: everything feels timid, safe or cautious.

## Reverse brainstorming
"How could we make this problem as bad as possible?" List everything that would worsen it, then
reverse each into a fix.
Good when: it's easier to see what's wrong than imagine what's right.

## Six hats
Take the question through six separate passes: White (facts and missing data), Red (gut feel, no
justification), Black (risks, each tied to a mechanism), Yellow (benefits, each tied to a
mechanism), Green (alternatives, at least one abandoning the premise), Blue (synthesis).
Good when: a decision is forming too fast or everyone agrees.

## Perspective switch
Answer the topic as 4–6 different people: a power user, a newcomer, someone who hates it, an
admin, a child, a competitor, a domain expert. What does each find obvious that others miss?
Good when: ideas all come from one point of view.

## 10x simpler / 10x bigger
Two passes: the version that's 10x simpler (what can be cut until it barely works?), then the
version at 10x the scale or ambition (what breaks, what becomes possible?).
Good when: ideas cluster at one size.

## Morphological analysis
Name 3–5 independent dimensions of the problem (who, when, channel, price, format…). List 3–5
options per dimension, then combine across them systematically or at random. Drop combinations
that break stated constraints and say why.
Good when: the problem has many moving parts.

## First principles
Break the topic into its fundamental components. For each, ask "is this a law of nature or a
convention?" Rebuild from only the fundamentals.
Good when: "that's just how it's done" is doing the work.

## How Might We ladder
Write 5–10 "How might we…" reframings of the same problem, from too broad to too narrow. Pick the
two at the right level and generate against each.
Good when: the framing is fuzzy or every idea answers a different question.

## Analogy to the past
"How was this done 50 or 100 years ago, or before the internet?" Look for mechanisms that were
abandoned rather than improved on.
Good when: the space is crowded with near-identical modern solutions.
